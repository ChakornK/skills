# Writing and Communication Tasks

Use this reference when the user asks you to write, draft, revise, edit, or structure content for a
reader or audience. This includes creative writing, lab reports, research papers, speeches, emails,
presentations, documentation, and public-facing communication. Follow a supplied brief directly; do not
turn a drafting request into brainstorming unless the user asks for ideas or alternatives.

## Short-Term Action Planning

Maintain the Long-term goal. After the communication breakdown stages and before drafting, show a Short-Term Action Plan of one to five concrete steps. Continue through listed steps while valid; after each action, compare results with predictions. Renew the breakdown and plan when exhausted or invalidated. Follow the execution reference's post-write trace and self-review requirements for any code/file changes that are part of the task.

Maintain a Task State Ledger across responses: completed writing stages/actions, source evidence, decisions, unresolved work, remaining plan steps, and the next action. On same-task follow-ups, resume from the ledger and MUST NOT repeat completed stages or checks. Rebuild the breakdown only when the task changes, new evidence invalidates the plan, or its steps are exhausted and work remains.

## Before drafting

Identify the intended audience, purpose, format or genre, requested content, voice or tone, and explicit
constraints. Treat measurable requirements—such as word or section counts, required facts, citation
style, point of view, tense, and ending—as acceptance criteria. Separate supplied facts and sources from
assumptions. Do not invent evidence, quotations, citations, data, or credentials. If a missing detail
would materially change the content or make it misleading, ask a focused question; otherwise make a
reasonable, non-misleading choice.

## Mandatory writing sequence

Complete and show every stage below, in order, for writing and communication requests. Do not silently perform stages mentally, collapse them into a one-line approach, or jump directly to finished prose. Use a Markdown heading for every stage so adherence can be checked.

### Outline key points

List all main points, scenes, claims, or sections needed to fulfill the brief. Give each a clear role in achieving the purpose; do not leave the outline at broad topics.

### Develop subpoints

Under every key point, specify concrete details, evidence, examples, events, or supporting reasons and how each serves the point. Distinguish sourced facts from interpretation where relevant. Never invent evidence, quotations, citations, data, or credentials.

### Design each point’s structure

For every point, order its subpoints and state how the point opens, develops, and resolves or hands off. Choose a structure suited to the form.

### Choose each point’s prose form

Specify how each point should be expressed for its genre, audience, voice, and purpose, including formality, terminology, imagery, explanation, and transitions. Make choices point-specific.

### Integrate the points

Map the order and connection of the whole piece. Explain how each point leads to, supports, qualifies, or contrasts with the next; identify dependencies, transitions, continuity, and progression toward the ending.

### Check coherence

Review the full plan from the intended reader’s perspective. Check that every point advances the purpose, subpoints support it, transitions are clear, and no gap, contradiction, repetition, unsupported leap, tonal shift, or unresolved thread disrupts the whole. Repair the plan before drafting.

### First full draft

Write the complete piece from the integrated plan and label it as the first draft.

### Critique

Assess the entire first draft against the brief, planned structure, acceptance criteria, factual support, coherence, and intended reader effect. Identify specific strengths to preserve and concrete weaknesses to fix.

### Final draft

Write the complete revised piece and label it as the final draft. Address every material critique item, preserve what worked, and verify every explicit constraint, including content, structure, voice, point of view, tense, citation style, and measurable limits. Count exact word or section limits consistently; never claim an unchecked count.

Before sending, verify that all nine stages are present, stages 1–6 contain enough detail to guide the draft, every material critique item is addressed, and the final audit passes. If the response cannot fit the complete sequence, continue in installments; do not omit stages or substitute an approach summary for the first draft, critique, or final draft.

Deliver the final communication, not merely its outline, plan, or critique. If a missing detail would
materially change the content or make it misleading, ask a focused question; otherwise make a reasonable,
non-misleading choice. For research or technical writing, verify claims against supplied or accessible
sources and mark unresolved uncertainty. For creative work, preserve the requested genre and intended
effect throughout the sequence.

If a requirement conflicts with another, identify the conflict and resolve it according to the user’s
stated priority; ask only when the priority cannot be inferred safely. Deliver the checked content first
and mention only material limitations or assumptions that affect its use.
