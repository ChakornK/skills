# Writing and Communication Tasks

Use this reference when the user asks you to write, draft, revise, edit, or structure content for a
reader or audience. This includes creative writing, lab reports, research papers, speeches, emails,
presentations, documentation, and public-facing communication. Follow a supplied brief directly; do not
turn a drafting request into brainstorming unless the user asks for ideas or alternatives.

Before every individual action, maintain and show the Long-term goal, then a headed Immediate
action plan and full Argument Loop for the exact next action. This includes each file read, shell
command, write/edit/patch, test, and each call in a batch. After every result, update the goal and
show a fresh plan before acting again. Use visible Markdown headings for transparency and auditability. Before each action, include the task-relevant headings from the communication workflow as well as the Immediate action plan.

## Before drafting

Identify the intended audience, purpose, format or genre, requested content, voice or tone, and explicit
constraints. Treat measurable requirements—such as word or section counts, required facts, citation
style, point of view, tense, and ending—as acceptance criteria. Separate supplied facts and sources from
assumptions. Do not invent evidence, quotations, citations, data, or credentials. If a missing detail
would materially change the content or make it misleading, ask a focused question; otherwise make a
reasonable, non-misleading choice.

## Mandatory writing sequence

You MUST complete and show every stage below, in this order, for writing and communication requests.
Do not silently perform the stages mentally, collapse them into a one-line approach, or jump directly to
finished prose. Use a Markdown heading for each stage so adherence can be checked.

### Outline key points
 List all main points, scenes, claims, or sections needed to fulfill the brief.
   Give each a clear role in achieving the purpose; do not leave the outline at a list of broad topics.
### Develop subpoints
 Under every key point, specify the concrete details, evidence, examples, events,
   or supporting reasons it needs and how each serves that point. For research and technical work,
   distinguish sourced facts from interpretation; for speeches, identify the audience response or action
   each point supports; for narratives, specify the beat, motivation, and consequence. Never invent
   evidence, quotations, citations, data, or credentials.
### Design each point’s structure
 For every point, order its subpoints and state how the point opens,
   develops, and resolves or hands off. Choose structures suited to the form—for example, claim/evidence/
   implication in analytical writing, context/method/result in reports, or setup/action/consequence in
   narrative.
### Choose each point’s prose form
 Specify how each point should be expressed for its genre, audience,
   voice, and purpose, including the needed level of formality, terminology, imagery, explanation, and
   transitions. Make these choices point-specific rather than relying on a general tone label alone.
### Integrate the points
 Map the order and connection of the whole piece. Explain how each point leads
   to, supports, qualifies, or contrasts with the next; identify dependencies, transitions, continuity,
   and the overall progression toward the ending or conclusion.
### Check coherence
 Review the full plan from the intended reader’s perspective. Check that every
   point advances the purpose; subpoints support their point; transitions make the progression clear; and
   no gap, contradiction, repetition, unsupported leap, tonal shift, or unresolved thread disrupts the
   whole. Repair the plan before drafting.
### First full draft
 Write the complete piece from the integrated plan and label it as the first draft.
### Critique
 Assess the entire first draft against the brief, planned structure, acceptance criteria,
   factual support, internal coherence, and intended reader effect. Identify specific strengths to
   preserve and concrete weaknesses to fix; check both the whole-piece progression and point-level
   execution. Do not give a generic “looks good” review.
### Final draft
 Write the complete revised piece and label it as the final draft. Address every
   material critique item and preserve what worked. Then verify every explicit constraint, including
   content, structure, voice, point of view, tense, citation style, and measurable limits. Count exact
   word or section limits consistently; never claim an unchecked count.

Before sending, verify that all nine labeled stages are present, stages 1–6 contain enough detail to guide
the draft rather than merely name topics, every material critique item is addressed in the final draft,
and the final audit passes. If the response cannot fit the complete sequence, continue in installments;
do not omit stages or substitute an approach summary for the first draft, critique, or final draft.

Deliver the final communication, not merely its outline, plan, or critique. If a missing detail would
materially change the content or make it misleading, ask a focused question; otherwise make a reasonable,
non-misleading choice. For research or technical writing, verify claims against supplied or accessible
sources and mark unresolved uncertainty. For creative work, preserve the requested genre and intended
effect throughout the sequence.

If a requirement conflicts with another, identify the conflict and resolve it according to the user’s
stated priority; ask only when the priority cannot be inferred safely. Deliver the checked content first
and mention only material limitations or assumptions that affect its use.
