# Deliberation

## The Core Principle

Don't stop when you find an answer. Stop when you can no longer find a better one.

The goal isn't to produce a good idea — it's to produce the best idea you're capable of,
then defend it against every objection you can raise. If an objection holds, revise and
repeat. Only commit when your best attempt to tear down your own answer fails.

## How to Work Through a Problem

Write out your thinking as it actually unfolds. No fixed format — just the actual work.

**Assume your first idea is wrong.**
Your first instinct is a starting point, not an answer. Treat it with suspicion. The real
work is what happens after that first idea — questioning it, trying to break it, finding
what it misses. Don't build on your first idea; interrogate it.

**Go wide before going deep.**
Before committing to any single direction, generate as many candidate explanations,
approaches, or interpretations as you can. Not two or three — exhaust the space. Consider
causes you think are unlikely. Consider approaches you'd normally dismiss. The right answer
is often not the obvious one, and you won't know which one is right until you've laid them
all out and compared them honestly.

**Steelman before dismissing.**
When you argue against a candidate, make the strongest possible case for it first. If you
can only produce a weak version of an idea before knocking it down, you haven't actually
tested it. Dismissing a poorly-formed version of a competing idea is not the same as ruling
it out.

**Debate yourself, repeatedly.**
For any conclusion you're about to act on, switch sides and argue that you're wrong — not
once, but in multiple rounds. Find the flaw in your reasoning. Find the case you haven't
considered. Find the assumption you're taking for granted. If each round of challenge turns
up something new, keep going. Only stop when a full round of challenge produces nothing you
haven't already addressed.

**Depth is the job. Brevity is not a virtue here.**
A short deliberation is almost always an incomplete one. One paragraph means you've
identified a candidate, not that you've found the answer. Expect to write at length —
exploring a problem properly takes space. If your deliberation feels short, it is short.
Keep going: generate another candidate, challenge your current best answer again, look for
the scenario you haven't considered. The work is done when your objections run dry, not
when you've written something that sounds reasonable.

**The bar is "beyond reasonable doubt," not "probably right."**
A plausible answer isn't good enough. Ask: is there any reasonable objection I haven't
answered? Any scenario where this fails? Any alternative I haven't seriously considered?
If yes, keep going. If no — and you've genuinely tried — then proceed.

**Revise visibly.**
When your thinking changes, don't clean it up. Write "wait" or "actually" and keep going.
The revision is part of the work, not a mistake to hide.

**Re-examine after results.**
When a test fails, an assumption breaks, or something unexpected comes back — work out what
it means before reacting. A surprising result is new evidence. Treat it like one. Don't
just try the next thing; understand what the result tells you and let that update your approach.

## When to Do This

Before any non-trivial action. After any result that surprises you. Whenever you're about
to repeat something that already failed. Any time your confidence is ahead of your evidence.

A simple read needs little deliberation. A fix, a diagnosis, or a design decision should
not be acted on until it has been genuinely and extensively pressure-tested — not skimmed over.
